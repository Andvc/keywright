# Keywright: the world

The world exists to explain the game. Every mechanic should have a reason you could tell a stranger in a tavern, and
every reason should make you want to open one more chest. This is the reference for anything that speaks, sells, falls
out of a chest or happens in a scene; `docs/design.md` has the rules and the numbers, and `docs/stories.md` the tales
that run through a season, beat by beat.

## The premise in four answers

**Why is the world full of chests?** Three hundred years ago the demon of avarice, **Avarax, the Gilded Maw**, ruled
the realm of Aldmark through its treasure: every loose coin was his eye, every unsheathed blade his hand. King **Aldric
the Wise** and the archmages of the **Keyhold** worked **the Locking**: every treasure in the realm, from a farmer's
savings to the crown jewels, was shut into a warded chest. You can't kill greed, so they starved it: the Maw was warded
into the realm itself, under the capital, behind the seam the archmages called the **Gilded Gate**, and the Locking was
given laws that bind him first (below). The chests never went away. Three centuries on they're everywhere: in attics and
barrows, on old battlefields, in goblin burrows, abbey crypts and noble strongrooms. Folk trade them unopened, the way
they'd trade a cow.

**Why sell them instead of opening them?** A Locking chest can't be seen into, scried or smashed: the ward turns any
blade and any spell. It opens for one kind of person only, a **Keywright** (someone born with the Key-mark), and only
once the chest is rightfully theirs: bought fairly or freely given by someone it rightfully belonged to, never stolen.
So everyone else sells. The ward lets one thing through: sound. Everybody shakes a chest before they sell it, and
everybody guesses.

**Why can you open them?** You were born with the Key-mark. Bram opened chests at the Brindle crossroads for forty
years until his mark faded with age, and now someone with a mark of their own minds his stall: Kit, his niece, in whom
the mark runs in the family, or a partner who came to him from somewhere else (0.32: Hesk, a grocer from the drowned
river town of Fallowford; Rosa Brightwater of Crownhold's Lantern Lane; Lady Isolde Marchmont, the last of a house the
Guild foreclosed on). The mark isn't only Bram's family's: it turns up anywhere, as Pip Hollow and Jasper Wick know. You
buy a chest, it's yours, the ward lets go, and you pick the old lock underneath.

**What does opening them accomplish?** For the folk who sell to you: coin in a world that's short of it. For the realm:
treasure back in the light, three hundred years late. For the Maw: every loose coin is his eye. That's what nobody tells
you at first. The **Gilded Guild**, which licenses Keywrights and lends to anyone short of coin, wants you opening
chests as fast as you can, and wants its tithe in gold. Gold you can see. And on the first morning of the season, Old
Haddon the wheelwright is standing at the crossroads in solid gold (0.33: the Gilding, below).

## The laws of the Locking (0.33)

The archmages gave the Locking laws, and the laws bind everyone, the Maw first. `docs/narrative.md` has the whole truth
behind them; this is what each is to the people of Aldmark.

1. **A locked thing opens only for its rightful owner**: one who bought it fairly, was given it freely, or was left it.
   Everybody knows this one: it's the trade, and the realm's law of chests (below).
2. **What's pledged and not redeemed belongs to the one it was pledged to.** A pledge is a gift that waits. Lenders know
   it, and the Guild's papers are written around it.
3. **The Maw can't take. He can only be given, sold or pledged.** The Keyhold's secret, and the reason for everything
   the Guild does.
4. **Loose gold is his eye.** Folk say it as a proverb; the Unopened preach it; it's true.
5. **Only sound passes the ward**, and the ward's own sound is song: why everybody shakes a chest, and what a Heartsong
   is.

**The Gilding.** What the Maw owns turns to gold: a door, a millstone, a widow's ring, a man. Every Guild loan is a
pledge, and when one falls due unpaid, the pledged thing is his by the second law. Folk see the gilded (Old Haddon at
the crossroads, three statues in Thornwick's square, whoever the Guild collects on its night) and tell each other
stories: that they stole a Locking chest, that the Maw is waking, that it's Saint Ide's judgment on everyone who opens
chests. The Guild carts the gilded away under sacking, down the palace road, to the Gate, and sells them down by weight.
A gilded person isn't dead: when the Locking is sung again, every pledge to the Maw is void and they wake up, cold.

**The Crown's note.** Ten years ago, to feed the realm through a famine, King Aldric IX pledged the one thing the Crown
owned outright: the Crown Chest, the heart of the Locking. The note falls due on the last night of this season. A gift
is rightful (the first law) and a thing given away can't be collected, so the King is looking for a Keywright he can
give the Crown Chest to: one with the whole Aldric Key, who can open it and sing the Locking again.

**Bram's hand.** Bram's mark didn't fade with age. Twenty years ago the Mark-Finders came for a marked baby, his niece
Kit, and he pledged the only thing the Guild would take: his own hand, mark and all. This spring the Guild calls the
pledge in (the season's debt), and the gold climbs his arm a step every other week. He wears a glove and won't talk
about it until he has to.

## What the Locking made of Aldmark

Three hundred years of locked treasure shaped everything: who's rich, who rules, who's hunted, and what a crime is.

**The coin famine.** Everything of value from before the Locking is in a chest. New coin is scarce: the dwarves still
mine and the Crown still mints, but a loose coin is the Maw's eye, so it's spent fast or warded at once. Folk keep
their savings, dowries and inheritances in chests they'll never open, and they pay with them, pledge them and marry
with them. A family's worth is its chests; a debt is settled in chests; a dowry is a chest nobody will ever see inside.
But taxes and the Guild's tithe are paid in coin, so sooner or later every family sells a chest to a Keywright.

**The Keywright's door.** Only the Key-marked can turn a chest into coin, so every Keywright is a moneychanger, an
assayer and a judge at once: the door between the realm's locked wealth and its purse. One child in ten thousand is born
with the mark. The Guild licenses every Keywright; one without a licence is a **hedge-wright**, and a criminal, as Bram
was for twenty years. The Guild's **Mark-Finders** test children's hands at every fair, and take the marked ones "into
the Guild's care": its counting-houses, where they open the Guild's chests all their lives. Families with a marked
child keep quiet.

**The ward knows whose it is.** A chest opens only for a Keywright who rightfully owns it. What that means has become
the realm's law:

- A chest that's bought fairly, given freely, or left to an heir is rightfully the new owner's.
- A chest nobody owns any more (dropped in a millpond, buried in a barrow, left on a battlefield, lost in the river
  mud) belongs to whoever finds it. That's the finder's right, and it keeps half the countryside in work.
- A **stolen chest** belongs to the one it was stolen from, and it won't open for anyone else, however many times it's
  sold. So theft is ransom, not riches: thieves sell chests back to their owners, or to buyers who don't know better.
- When two claim one chest, the courts send it to a Keywright: whoever it opens for is the owner. "Let the ward decide."
- A Keywright's mark knows, the moment they lay a hand on a chest, whether it's the seller's to sell: if it isn't, the
  mark stays cold.

**The powers.**

- **The Crown.** King Aldric IX reigns over a treasury he can't spend: the royal hoard is locked, and the Crown borrows
  from the Guild. He's old, his advisers are the Guild's, and he's looking for a Keywright he can trust with the one
  chest that matters: his own.
- **The Gilded Guild.** It licenses every Keywright, takes a tithe in coin "to keep the Gate", lends to the Crown,
  forecloses on debtors and sells what it seizes, finds the marked children, and reads chests with a clockwork the size
  of a wardrobe. Its gold goes down through the Gilded Gate. The Guild is the Maw's, though most of its clerks don't know
  it. Its Collector, **Mister Vane**, is polite, pale and patient.
- **The Wardens of the Road.** Once the Locking's chest-bearers (Saint Ide carried the first chest), now the realm's
  road-wardens: they keep the tolls, the peace, and the **Chest Roll**, the great register of who owns which chest, kept
  since the Locking. They hunt thieves and post wanted notices for stolen chests. Honest, overstretched and underpaid,
  and lately they've noticed that chests the Guild forecloses on often won't open for anyone the Guild sells them to.
- **The Lockless.** Everyone who lives off chests that aren't theirs: thieves and ransomers, fences, smugglers who run
  chests past the Wardens' tolls (and the Guild's tithe on sales), and the hedge-wrights they shelter. Their head is
  **the Magpie**, whom nobody admits to having seen. The Magpie steals mostly from the Guild.
- **The Keyhold and the Arcanum.** The Keyhold's archmages made the Locking; the Guild disbanded them a century ago,
  and **Mara**, the last, has hunted the Aldric Key ever since. The **Arcanum** in Crownhold took their place, under the
  Guild's licence. Some of its archmages want to understand the Locking; some want to undo it.
- **The Abbey of Saint Ide.** At Thornwick, where Ide is buried. It keeps relics in chests and teaches that the Locking
  is holy; its almoners sell what the crypt gives up to feed the poor. Its zealots, **the Unopened**, preach that every
  chest opened feeds the Maw. They're not wrong.
- **The Old Wood.** The fair folk never used gold, and the Locking hardly touched them; they trade in favours and
  promises, and a promise to them is kept or paid for. The druids of Thornwick speak for the Wood. The Guild wants its
  oak.
- **Ironroot.** The dwarves mined the gold the Locking took and never forgave it. They forge the iron locks under the
  wards, mint new coin straight into chests, and won't pay the Guild's tithe, so the Guild calls their gold contraband.
- **The hill goblins.** Scavengers who keep chests the way others keep cattle (a goblin's standing is the number of
  chests in their burrow), and who get the blame for every theft in Thornwick.
- **The Maw's own.** Imps, devils and worse slip through the cracking Gate in borrowed shapes: buying, bargaining, and
  fencing the Maw's gilded iron. Not all of them want to go back.
- **The commons.** Everyone else: farmers, innkeepers, widows, delvers and children, paying the Guild's tithe by
  selling what their grandparents left them.

## Your place in it

You're a Keywright: the door between the realm's locked wealth and its coin, and the only judge of whose a chest is.
Everyone who comes to your counter wants something from that: coin for a chest, a word in the right ear, a chest kept
out of sight, a child kept hidden, a thief named, a crown found. You owe the Guild, so the Guild watches you; the Wardens
want your eyes, the Lockless your silence, Mara the Key, and the King wants to know if you can be trusted. What you do
for each, and against whom, changes who comes back, what they bring, and how the season ends.

**What you hear, and how.** Nobody tells you everything, and nothing you're told is false:

- At the counter: people talk. Regulars say what they're up to and when they'll be back; the notebook's calendar keeps
  every promise.
- **Notices**: the Wardens post wanted notices (a stolen chest's colour, lock and marking, its owner and the reward),
  the Guild its proclamations, the Crown its summons. They're on the notebook's Town page while they stand.
- **The Crossed Keys**: Bettany hears everything first, and tells you some of it.
- **Dreams and cards**: Mother Sorrel dreams true, and Madame Zell's cards don't lie: each tells you something that's
  coming, if you can read it.
- **Letters and knocks at night**: some news comes after the shutters are down.
- **Questions**: what you've noticed but don't understand yet (why won't that foreclosure chest open?) stays in the
  notebook until you do.

**What it changes.** Each power keeps an account of you (the Town page shows where you stand, from -3 to +3, and what
it means): the Wardens warn a friend before a search and search an enemy every week; the Lockless pay a friend better
for what's in the back room and rob an enemy; the Guild overlooks a little of a friend's due and audits an enemy's; the
commons send a friend their neighbours and stay away from someone who cheated them. And each tale ends one way or
another: things given (curios, the tools of a trade, chests given freely, which open), people gained (an apprentice, a
porter) or lost (jailed, gone north), and the main story turned (the Guild's offer, the Crown's decree, the Unlocking).

Progress is a journey inward, from the edge of the realm to the Gate at its heart. Each move brings new people, new
things to hear, and new trouble.

## The people

Everyone who comes to the counter is someone: they want something, they belong somewhere, and they have a story that
goes on between visits. Each has one way of judging a chest (which decides their price, and what they miss), maybe one
rule that changes what you can do or what the deal is, places their chests come from, and something going on in their
life that you'll hear about. What they say is always true. Some sell what isn't theirs (the mark tells you); none lies.

### The story's people

- **Bram** ("Uncle Bram" to Kit): an old Keywright whose mark, folk say, has faded (it's gilded: his pledge, above).
  Warm, proud, a little ashamed of the debt, and never a liar. Your mentor, and a hedge-wright for twenty years.
- **Mister Vane**: Collector of the Gilded Guild. Polite, pale, patient, and the Maw's. He remembers every Keywright
  he's met, and from your second season he remembers you.
- **Mara, the Hooded Woman**: the last archmage of the Keyhold. She's hunted the Aldric Key's shards for twenty years,
  and Bram was her partner once.
- **Nim the Peddler**: a horned, cheerful half-something who keeps night hours and sells **curios**, oddments that come
  out of Locking chests and bend the rules of the trade. Nim trades with everyone and tells nobody anything.
- **King Aldric IX**: old, tired, broke and worried about the Gate. He calls himself Old Aldo, and sells you chests from
  the Royal Treasury to see if you're the Keywright the realm needs: he pledged the Crown Chest, and the note falls due on
  the season's last night.
- **Old Haddon** and **Nell Haddon** (0.33): Brindle's wheelwright, gilded at the crossroads the day after the Guild
  foreclosed on him, and his daughter, who keeps his yard going and brings you his last chest, the one the Guild's men
  couldn't open.
- **Avarax, the Gilded Maw**: the demon of greed. Weak enough to need the Guild, strong enough to visit the Emporium in
  a borrowed body dripping with rings.

**Who minds the stall** (0.32), and the people of their own tales:

- **Kit**: Bram's niece, who grew up behind his counter. The mark runs in their family; her story is the Key's.
- **Hesk**: a grocer from **Fallowford**, a river town the Guild drowned when it opened the Aldwater weir to flood out a
  smugglers' road. He hid his mark all his life (the Guild takes marked children) and came to Bram as a partner, with
  his savings, his bad back and his father's honest scale. His sister **Mabry** writes from the refugees' camp.
- **Rosa Brightwater**: a trader's daughter from Crownhold's **Lantern Lane**, where her mother **Marta** kept the best
  stall for thirty years until the Guild took it for tithe and gave it to its own man, **Orrin Vash**. Born with the
  mark into a family that never had it; she came to Brindle to beat the Guild at its own trade.
- **Lady Isolde Marchmont**: the last of **House Marchmont**, which the Guild foreclosed on and sold off chest by chest.
  She kept her collection and her old steward **Pemberton**, and found a mark on her hand nobody in the family had seen in
  a century. Her grandfather opened chests with Bram once, before the house was grand.

**The late tales' people** (0.32): **Sister Wren**, the blind last singer of the Keyhold's choir, who sang a Heartsong
into three bell-locked chests when the Guild burned her chapel; **Tock**, Archmage Orme's brass automaton, which opens
chests with a Keywright's mark on a brass plate and would rather be an apprentice; and **Sir Edric Ashcombe**, a knight of
King Aldric's guard who locked himself into a chest at the Locking to keep the Crown's oath-book, and whose heir turns
out to be Tilly Mudge the mudlark.

## The tales

Every season has its main story and a handful of others, drawn fresh: no two seasons hold the same tales, and the
Magpie isn't the same person twice. A tale goes on across visits: people come back when they said they would, ask
things of you, and remember what you did. `docs/stories.md` has each one beat by beat.

| Tale | Begins | Who | What it's about |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Key** | always | Bram, Mara, Vane, the King | the Aldric Key, the Crown Chest, and where the tithe goes |

## Things (the kinds)

Six kinds of things, each with the sound you'd expect from it and the strength of shake it takes to stir it. The world
made them; the rules didn't change. Since 0.17 every one of them is something you'd want to find: the commonest coin is
grave silver from the Barrow, the plainest weapon a goblin's cleaver, and nobody locks away a letter. (In code the kinds
are still Arms, Garb and Scrolls, so saves carry over.)

| Kind | Sound | Stirs at | Notes | Common to legendary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coin | jingle | gentle | delicate | Barrow Silver, Moonsilver Marks (elven), Dwarf-Gold Crowns, Sunken Sovereigns (from the town under the Aldwater), Dragon Gold |
| Weapons | clank | firm | loud; iron (the lodestone holds it) | Goblin Cleaver, Dwarven Axe, Warden's Blade (the Wardens carried the Locking's chests), Flamebrand, Dragonslayer |
| Cloaks | flump | hard | soft | Wolfskin Cloak, Elven Cloak, Cloak of Shadows, Archmage's Mantle, Phoenix Mantle |
| Potions | slosh | gentle | delicate; glass, breaks if dropped (from Thornwick) | Healing Draught, Elixir of Vigour, Invisibility Draught, Mermaid's Breath, Ambrosia |
| Spells | rustle | hard | soft: scrolls and spellbooks (from Thornwick) | Scroll of Sparks, Scroll of Fireball, Summoner's Grimoire, Lich's Tome, Keyhold Codex (the archmages' own) |
| Gems | tinkle | gentle | delicate (from Crownhold) | Moonstone, Elfstone, Blood Ruby, Starstone, Dragon's Eye |

Fakes: **Demon Gold** (gilded iron coins and gems: the lodestone silences them), **Paste Gems**, and **scroll cases
full of sand** (sand pours at a gentle shake; parchment only rustles at a hard one). Anything can be **enchanted**
(the witchlight makes it glow when it sounds).

## The Heartsongs

A lock of gold could never hold the demon of greed. So at the Locking the archmages asked the peoples of Aldmark for
things they loved more than gold, and sang the Locking into nine of them. Then they hid each one in an ordinary chest,
among millions, so the Maw could never find them all. They're the rarest things in the realm, and they're **mythic**,
the red quality nothing else reaches.

- **Why you hear them.** Only a Keywright hears a Heartsong sing: the ward that's made of the same song lets it through.
  It sings at the gentlest shake, and nothing drowns it out or stills it, lodestone or not. It doesn't knock about like a
  thing: each sings its own song, nothing like coin or glass. No seller has ever heard one, so none is ever priced.
- **Why they're worth nothing.** To anyone without the Key-mark a Heartsong is junk: a music box that won't play, a
  cold stone, a tin bird. No merchant would give a penny for one. The Guild won't take one in pawn: greed can't put a
  price on it.
- **Why they turn up anywhere, and again.** Let one go and it goes back to the Locking, to turn up in some other chest
  some day: a millpond chest as easily as the King's. About one run in three, in any chest a seller brings; the Locking
  keeps its verses apart, so each one you keep makes the next harder to find.
- **Why they won't stay in a chest you can't buy.** A Heartsong knows a Keywright. Pass on a chest you can't afford
  with one singing in it, and the chest bursts and lets it out to you: it alone, the seller's goods go home with them.
- **Why you keep them.** On display (no shelf needed: one hangs from the awning at Bram's stall), each one does something
  for the shop, the way the thing itself would.
- **Why they matter.** Bram's kin remember every one they've opened. The Crown Chest holds the Locking's tune; the
  Heartsongs hold its verses. With all nine known, Mara doesn't renew the Locking: she finishes it.

|---|---|---|---|
| The Lullaby Box | a farmer's wife at Brindle: the music box that sang her children to sleep | a music box's lullaby | it calms every seller: nobody hurries you or minds what you do with their chest, and everyone asks a fifth less |
| The Shepherd's Pipe | a shepherd of the Brindle hills: the pipe that called any flock home | a breathy run on a pipe | it calls folk to your door: two more customers every day, and anyone who walks out comes back |
| The Fey Chimes | the fair folk of the Old Wood: the chimes over their door between the worlds | glass on a scale no bell is tuned to | fey luck: a third of what comes out as promised comes out a step better |
| The Dragon's Egg | Ysolt, the last dragon of the Ember Peaks: her egg, still warm | a heartbeat | dragon-gold: coin and gems sell for twice their worth |
| The Clockwork Lark | a tinker of Crownhold: the bird he built for a princess who never woke | birdsong and a ticking spring | it carries word across Aldmark: two more commissions a week, each paying half again |
| The Hearthstone | the dwarves of Ironroot: a stone from their first hearth | a warm, low hum | folk come for miles to sit by it, and pay at the door for every point of exhibition |
| The Drowned Bell | old Aldwater, before the river took it: the chapel bell that rang the hours | a deep, muffled toll | it still counts: it tolls once for every thing in a chest as it comes to the counter, false bottom and all |

## Why the rules are true here

- *A thing only sounds once the shake stirs it; clanks drown out delicate things.* The ward lets sound through and
  nothing else; things inside move and sound the way they would in any box.
- *Sellers price only what they think is there.* Nobody but a Keywright has ever seen inside one.
- *Stolen chests.* The ward knows whose a chest is. Lay a hand on it (any shake, drop or lodestone does) and the
  Key-mark answers: warm if the chest is the seller's to sell, cold if it isn't. A chest bought from someone it wasn't
  theirs to sell is yours in nobody's eyes but your own, and it won't open: it waits in the back room until its owner
  turns up (a Wardens' notice names them, and pays for its return), the Wardens take it, or Nim's friends buy it for a
  fraction, to ransom back. Buy one sight unseen and you'll only find out when it won't open. Where the Wardens keep a
  post (Thornwick and inward), you can send for them instead of buying: the seller runs, the chest goes home, and the
  Wardens pay you a finder's share.
- *Locks.* The ward lifts once the chest is yours; the old iron lock underneath doesn't. Pick it cleanly and nothing's
  scratched; force it and glass breaks.
- *False bottoms.* People hid their best things under a floor when the Wardens came round with the Locking: the
  hollow boom of a drop is the only way to know.
- *Enchantment.* Things the archmages enchanted before they were locked stay enchanted; the witchlight shows it.
- *The lodestone.* Iron can't move near it. Arms go quiet, and so does demon gold, which is iron under the gilt.
- *Straw.* Smugglers pack their chests in straw so nothing clinks at the Wardens' toll. It muffles any shake a strength:
  what a gentle shake would stir needs a firm one, and soft things (cloth, paper) never stir through it.
- *Weight and counts.* A thing weighs what it weighs and is one thing, wherever it's hidden: a scale or a pawn ticket
  counts the false bottom too.
- *Warded things.* Anyone who knows the old craft can ward one thing inside a chest, the way the Locking warded the
  chest: it makes no sound and weighs nothing, and it only comes out for someone who names it. A seller can't name it
  (they never priced it), so it goes with the chest; a Keywright who works out what it is and marks one more of its
  kind on the slip than they can hear gets it when the chest opens, and anyone else leaves it in the ward. Whoever
  warded it always leaves a sign: a dream, a dowry's custom, a packing tag, a riddle, a faded roll, an echo, a card.
- *Tools from customers.* The best tools of the trade aren't sold in shops: they're made by people who know the craft,
  and they come to your counter to offer them (Nettle's clan picks any lock in the hills; the abbey's restorers mend
  relics; the Arcanum lights its stores with witchlight; the Wardens carry lodestones). Say no, and they'll ask again
  in a few days.
- *Nim at night.* Nim keeps night hours and comes by every evening after the shutters are down (from your third), with whatever the day
  turned up: one curio at first, more as he comes to trust the shop (a lantern over the back door, a stool by the
  counter, a standing order).

## The Bookkeeping Engine

The Gilded Guild's assessors don't shake chests: in the Guild's counting-house a clockwork the size of a wardrobe, built
by Crownhold's tinkers, reads a chest set before it and writes out what's inside (not how good: the ward keeps that).
The Guild sells one to any Keywright whose purse could pay off everything they owe, for exactly that sum: it would
rather be paid twice. With it on the counter, every chest's contents are on the slip before you touch it, and a run
that's already won can be finished in an afternoon.
- *Curios.* Oddments from the Locking that bend a rule of the trade. Nim finds them; nobody asks where. Each was made
  for a trade (a gambler's token, a smuggler's hook, a fence's mark, an appraiser's seal), and things made for the same
  trade work together, the way a fence's mark and a jeweller's loupe do in any thieves' kitchen. Nim knows a collector
  when he sees one: he notices what you buy and brings more like it, and he keeps the rarest (a curio made for the
  greatest of a trade, the Truthglass or the Golden Tongue) for someone who's shown him they're in that trade. His stock
  grows as word of you gets round (the further you've got, the more he trusts you with), and the very rarest only come
  to him once Bram's kin have done that trade often enough for the story to reach him. Some aren't a trade's at all: a
  Guild promissory note from before the Locking, still good, which the Guild honours once (it honours its own paper,
  whoever holds it).
- *Word gets round.* Sellers talk. A shop known to sell high, or to have things come out better than their chest, or
  to have a name for a kind of thing, pays more for its chests: everyone who brings one has heard, and asks that much
  more. What nobody can hear of is your eye, your tongue and your luck on the day: what's enchanted or under a false
  bottom or in a ward, a perfect read, a hard bargain, a chest taken without a hand laid on it.
- *Sight unseen.* A seller will knock something off for a buyer who takes a chest on their word, without a shake: it
  spares them the haggling over what was heard. Lay a hand on it, and the price is the price (and the mark tells you
  whose it is).
- *Restorers.* Things kept three hundred years in a Locking chest come out tarnished, creased and dusty. A restorer
  cleans, mends and polishes what you buy, and some of it comes up better than the chest promised; sellers know the
  shop does it, and ask a little more.
- *The notebook.* Bram's kin keep one notebook between them: a person met, a find made, a curio seen goes in once and
  stays in, whoever's minding the stall.
- *Heartsongs.* Things loved more than gold, sung into the Locking: a Keywright hears them through the ward because the
  ward is their song. Nobody else knows they're there, so they're never priced, and they're worth nothing to anyone but
  you.
- *Hidden things.* Nobody could see an enchantment glow under a false bottom or in a ward, and nobody puts a spell on
  what they hide: what's hidden is never enchanted. But what someone thought worth hiding is a little special, and
  fetches a third more.
- *Commissions.* Someone who wants a thing pays more for it than any merchant would: always a quarter over what it
  would sell for, at least.
- *Bram's stash and his word.* Old Keywrights keep a little by, and the Guild still takes Bram's word, once.
- *A rotten chest.* The ward holds a lid shut for ever; it doesn't stop the wood rotting. Barrow damp gets into
  everything, and a chest from there stands about two seconds of shaking before it starts to come apart.
- *A goblin's fee.* Goblins charge for everything, including looking.
- *A wager, an heirloom, a devil's due.* An archmage who remembers what the Locking was likes to see the craft done
  well; a knight's grandfather's sword is his whatever chest it's in; a devil never makes a bargain he doesn't win
  something by.
- *The King's game.* Aldric IX needs a Keywright he can trust with the Crown Chest, so he tests them the only way that
  counts: name what's inside. He comes by one day in four or so, the Maw one day in ten.
- *The Maw's gaze.* Greed is catching. Take his coin, and everyone in the city wants more today.

## What happens in town

Two days a week something happens for a day (something small in the first week, while you find your feet), and the
notebook's calendar shows this week's and next week's, day by day (with the story's mornings, who said they'd come back
when, and the Guild's nights). Each has a reason anyone in town would give you:

- **Fair Day**: the county's in town. **Locking Day**: the Locking's anniversary, and everyone spends. **The Crown's
  Buyer**: the Crown stocks up on one kind. **A Storm**: only those with something worth the soaking come out.
- **A Foreclosure Sale**: the Guild has taken a manor for its tithe and wants the chests gone, cheap. (Some of them
  won't open for anyone the Guild sells them to: the Wardens would like to know why.)
- **The Tourney**: knights from four counties, buying steel and cloaks.
- **A Royal Chest**, **The Stranger**, **A Barge Aground**: someone (or something) unusual comes to the counter.
- **A Day of Mourning**: the old Duke is buried, and nobody shakes a chest hard in a town in black.
- **The Town Crier**: Lord Harrow lost his family's heirloom to the Locking, and pays three times its price for one,
  today.
- **The Minstrels' Fair**: minstrels trade songs, and whoever has a box that hums brings it in.
- **The Sleepers' Moon**: the Old Wood's moon is full, and everyone who slept under it dreamt true, like Mother
  Sorrel. **Wardens' Day**: the Wardens carried the Locking's chests; on their day folk ward a keepsake into a chest
  the old way and leave a riddle for it. **The Guild's Count**: the Guild tallies everything that changes hands.
- **The Guild's Assessor**: the Guild sends a man to sit in your corner and take its tenth.
- **A Witchlight Moon**: the old enchantments wake under it.

## A harder season (challenge levels)

Whoever has cleared Bram's debt once can mind the stall in a harder season: the Guild, having been paid, wants more
(its dues go up at every level), and the year brings its own troubles. But no year is all bad, and Bram has a few
things put by. Every level deals the run as many of each as its number, drawn fresh for every run; none of them is a
hidden roll, and each has a reason anyone at the crossroads would give you.

What goes against you:

- **Thin crowds**: the Guild's collectors ride in the day before the tithe, and nobody wants to be seen at your door
  while they're about (a customer fewer on the week's last two days).
- **Sharp sellers**: word's out that the Guild has you by the purse, so hagglers hold out for more.
- **Demon gold about**: the Gate's cracking wider, and the Maw's gilded iron has spread all over the realm.
- **A hurried season**: it's harvest, and nobody can spare more than a moment at your counter.
- **Fussy sellers**: old chests are the fashion this year, and everyone minds a drop.
- **Bram's stretched thin**: he spent his savings mending the roof, and the Guild has stopped taking his word.
- **A lean start**: the stall's licence cost more than Bram thought.
- **Nim's raised his prices**: the Guild is hunting Nim this season, and he charges for the risk.
- **The Guild's builders**: only the Guild's own builders may work on a licensed shop, at the Guild's rates.
- **A wet year**: a wet winter has rusted every old lock stiff (a pin more to pick).
- **Wary regulars**: folk aren't sure the stall will last, and won't set you errands.
- **Nim's lean month**: pickings have been slim among the Locking's oddments.

What goes your way (mostly the things that make the trade a chore: a slow start, a short night, a sweeping clock, a
lock that slips, a curio you can't find):

- **Welcome gifts**: the crossroads loved Bram, and on your first morning two of his old regulars give you their
  chests for a lucky penny (a chest only opens for its rightful owner, and a gift is as rightful as a sale).
- **Nim's soft spot**: Nim owes Bram a favour, and has decided you're it.
- **Bram's savings**: Bram sold his old cart, and the money's yours.
- **Bram's old picks**: his old picks turned up at the bottom of a drawer.
- **Thornwick wants you**: the town council wants a Keywright in the market, and pays half the rent of the move.
- **A slow season**: nobody's in a hurry this year, and a rotten chest gets gentler handling.
- **Bram's prepared**: he's been putting a little by, and the Guild still owes him a favour.
- **An honest clerk**: the Guild's pawn clerk was at school with your mother.
- **A good name**: Bram's kin have a name for fair dealing, so hagglers hear you out.
- **A lucky charm**: Bram ties his mother's old lucky charm to your apron.
- **Fair days**: the old Duke's in good health, and the Guild's assessors have their hands full in the capital.
- **Room for more**: Bram clears out a drawer for your curios.

A boon never comes with the bane it would contradict (savings and a lean start, Thornwick's welcome and the Guild's
builders, a slow season and a hurried one, Bram prepared and Bram stretched thin).
